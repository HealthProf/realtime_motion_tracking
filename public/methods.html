Methods and limitations

This is a teaching tool. It has not been validated against laboratory motion capture. Report its numbers as estimates and describe the setup you used.

What the app measures

A pose-estimation neural network (MediaPipe Pose Landmarker, BlazePose architecture; Bazarevsky et al., 2020) finds 33 body keypoints in each camera frame. Everything else is computed from those keypoints. Video never leaves your device.

Keypoints are the model's estimate of where a joint appears in the image. They are not palpated anatomical landmarks or joint centres. The hip is typically the least certain, and the far-side limb in a side view is partly guessed while it is hidden.

The planar (2D) assumption

Angles and distances are measured in the image plane. They equal the true values only when the movement takes place in a plane perpendicular to the camera's line of sight. If a limb points toward or away from the camera, its projected angle is wrong and its projected length is too short. The setup coach warns when the body is turned obliquely, but it cannot correct the error.

  • Use a side (sagittal) view for flexion and extension, and a front view for abduction and adduction.
  • Place the camera level, at about hip height, far enough away that the whole movement stays in frame (3 to 4 m is typical). Greater distance reduces perspective distortion.
  • Keep the subject near the centre of the picture, where lens distortion is smallest.

The "3D (experimental)" angles use depth that the model infers from appearance. Depth is not measured, its error is not well characterised, and these values should not be treated as equivalent to the planar measurements.

Calibration

Angles need no calibration. Lengths, velocities and accelerations are in pixels until you calibrate. Clicking the two ends of an object of known length gives a scale that is valid only at that object's distance from the camera, so put it in the plane of motion. The standing-height option assumes eye level is 93.6 % of stature (Drillis & Contini, 1966) and is less accurate; prefer a reference object for lab data.

The coordinate system has x to the right and y upward, with the origin at the bottom-left of the picture unless you move it.

Timing

Each frame carries its own timestamp from the camera pipeline; the app does not assume a fixed frame rate. Webcams usually deliver 30 frames per second and slow down in dim light. Fast movements (throwing, kicking, sprinting, landings) are undersampled and blurred at these rates. The export header reports the median, minimum and maximum frame interval so you can judge timing quality.

Filtering and differentiation

StageMethod
Live displayOne Euro filter (Casiez et al., 2012), a causal adaptive low-pass filter. Live values lag slightly and are labelled "live estimate".

Angles are computed from filtered coordinates.
Velocity, accelerationCentral finite differences of the filtered data.

The default cutoff is 6 Hz, a common choice for gait and other moderate-speed movements. It is a choice, not a fact: the "Suggest cutoff" button runs a residual analysis (Winter, 2009), and the "Why filtering matters" panel shows the consequences. Each differentiation amplifies noise. In this app's own tests with half a pixel of keypoint noise at 60 Hz, joint angle error was a fraction of a degree, angular velocity error was a few percent of peak, and angular acceleration error at a 6 Hz cutoff was a large fraction of peak for a slow squat. Treat acceleration as indicative unless the movement is slow, the frame rate is high and the cutoff is well chosen. Real keypoint noise is usually larger than in those tests.

Angle definitions

Joint angles are computed from three keypoints. "Included" is the geometric angle at the middle keypoint. "Anatomical" reports flexion as 180° minus the included angle for the knee, hip and elbow (0° is straight). The hip angle is the trunk-thigh angle (shoulder-hip-knee) and so includes pelvic and lumbar motion. The shoulder angle is the arm-trunk angle. The ankle angle uses the toe keypoint, whose neutral offset is approximate. Segment angles are measured counter-clockwise from the right horizontal; trunk lean is measured from vertical. The exact definition of every exported angle is written in the CSV header.

Centre of mass

The centre of mass is estimated by the segmental method with the mass fractions and segment centre-of-mass positions of de Leva (1996). The keypoints do not coincide with de Leva's segment endpoints, so three approximations are made: the head and neck centre of mass is placed at the midpoint of the ears; the trunk runs from mid-shoulder to mid-hip; the hand runs from wrist to index knuckle. A hidden limb segment is replaced by the opposite side, which is flagged in the export. The result is a model estimate for an average body.

Repetitions, symmetry and jump height

  • Repetitions are excursions of at least 15° away from and back to the resting angle, found with hysteresis thresholds and extended to the turning points.
  • Symmetry index = 100 × (left − right) / mean(left, right) (Robinson et al., 1987). It is reported only for front views; in a side view the far limb is partly hidden and at a different distance from the camera.
  • Jump height from flight time: h = g t² / 8. This needs no calibration but assumes the body is in the same position at take-off and landing; landing with bent knees inflates it. Flight time is resolved to about one frame interval, which the results table converts to a height resolution (about ±4 cm at 30 frames per second for a 0.5 s flight). The centre-of-mass method is shown beside it for comparison.

Anatomy layers

Bones, muscles, internal organs, arteries, veins, nerves, joints and ligaments, lymphatics and surface landmarks are simplified schematics drawn from the tracked keypoints. They show the typical location of structures in an average adult. They are not an image of the subject.

  • Limb structures lie between two tracked joints, so their position is reasonably constrained. Rotation about a segment's long axis (for example forearm pronation) cannot be shown.
  • Organs and trunk vessels are placed from only four keypoints (shoulders and hips) at textbook positions. Real positions shift with breathing, posture and body build, and differ between people. Use the layer for "roughly where", nothing more precise.
  • Blood flow is an animation of direction: away from the heart in arteries, toward it in veins. Its rate is a fixed nominal value. It is not this person's pulse, and the camera cannot see circulation.
  • Muscles are not shown as active or inactive. The "muscles crossing" highlight is a fixed anatomical lookup.
  • Reproductive organs are omitted because the tool has no information about the subject's sex. Left and right are the subject's own left and right in every view.
  • In a side view, paired structures are drawn once, for the side nearer the camera.

Finding a structure by name

Search matches only the structures in this app's atlas (about 120, including common names such as "kneecap"). If the structure is in the picture it is circled. If the part of the body it belongs to is hidden or tracked with low confidence, the circle is dashed and labelled as uncertain. If it lies outside the picture, an arrow points toward it. That position is extrapolated by the pose model beyond the edge of the image, so the direction is dependable but the distance is not. If the shoulders and hips are not tracked, the app says it cannot locate the structure.

Bike fit mode

For a rider on a stationary trainer. Record 20 to 30 seconds of steady pedalling, square-on from the side (camera level with the bottom bracket), and separately from the front if you want to compare knee tracking. Measurements are averaged over pedal revolutions and shown as mean, standard deviation and count.

A value is called out of range only when it is beyond the limit by more than 5°, the assumed uncertainty of this tool (published 2D errors for this kind of pose model are about 5° or more). Values just outside are "borderline", and the app suggests confirming with a goniometer. Averaging many revolutions reduces random error but not a consistent offset, such as a hip keypoint that sits in the wrong place for the whole trial. Suggestions are phrased as options to try and re-measure, and each trial is compared with the previous one in the same view.

A bike frame, handlebars and the far leg hide parts of the body, and the pose model was not trained specifically on cyclists. When a keypoint needed for a measurement is tracked for less than 80 % of the trial, that measurement is withheld.

Running gait mode

For a runner on a treadmill, after several minutes of settling in. The set of measurements follows the 2D video framework of Souza (2016, doi:10.1016/j.pmr.2015.08.006). That paper is explicit on two points that govern how this mode words its output: no cutoffs for abnormal running kinematics exist, and recommending form changes to uninjured runners is not evidence-based.

  • Contact and toe-off are taken as the furthest-forward position of the heel and furthest-back position of the toe relative to the pelvis, adapted from a method validated for walking (Zeni et al., 2008, doi:10.1016/j.gaitpost.2007.07.007). It has not been validated for running, and in this tool it has been tested only on synthetic data. Unfiltered positions are used for the events because filtering lengthened the apparent contact time by about 30 ms in testing.
  • Timing (contact time, flight time, duty factor) is resolved to about one frame. At 30 frames per second that is 33 ms, over a tenth of a typical ground contact. Use 60 frames per second or more. Step rate assumes left and right steps are alike.
  • Foot-strike angle is the foot's angle to the ground at contact minus its angle when flat. It is classed as rearfoot above 8°, forefoot below −1.6°, midfoot between (Altman & Davis, 2012, doi:10.1016/j.gaitpost.2011.09.104). Those thresholds came from 9 midfoot strikes in 20 runners using marker-based capture at 1000 Hz. Here the flat reference is taken at mid-stance instead of standing, and the heel and toe keypoints are among the least certain, so treat the class as approximate. Strike pattern is reported for description only: the framework paper finds limited evidence that any pattern is more injurious.
  • Peak knee flexion in stance is compared with "about 45° typical, under 40° associated with some patellofemoral pain subgroups" from the same framework.
  • Step-rate option. When the shank is angled forward at contact with a heel-first landing, the app offers a 5 to 10 % higher step rate as something to try, based on Heiderscheit et al. (2011, doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181ebedf4), who changed step rate relative to each runner's own preferred rate. There is no universal target such as 180 steps per minute.
  • Back view reports pelvic drop on each side, step width, knee separation and trunk side lean. The framework paper notes that 2D pelvic drop is repeatable but agrees poorly with 3D measurement, so use it for left/right and before/after comparison only. Keypoints are joint centres, so the "knee window" of the framework cannot be judged directly.

Neither mode can assess pain, diagnose injury, or tell you the right position or technique for an individual. Neither has been compared with a professional fitting or gait-analysis system.

Calibration from standing height (countdown)

The rough calibration compares the eye-to-heel distance in the picture with eye height, taken as 93.6 % of stature (Drillis & Contini, 1966). A countdown gives the person time to walk to where they will move; when it ends, about 0.7 s of frames are collected and the scale is accepted only if the eyes and both heels are visible, the whole body is in the picture, the person is still, the knees and hips are straight and the trunk is upright. Otherwise it is refused with the reason. The origin is moved to the floor under the feet.

Limits: the 93.6 % figure is a population average, the eye and heel keypoints are model estimates, and the scale holds only at the distance from the camera where the person stood. Expect an error of a few percent, which carries straight through to every length, velocity, force and power. An object of known length in the plane of motion is better; for barbell lifts the plate itself is the best reference.

Video files

A recorded video can be used instead of the camera. It never leaves the device. There are two ways to use it:

  • Play through: the file plays and is measured as it goes, like the live camera. If the pose model is slower than the playback, frames are skipped.
  • Analyse every frame: the app steps through the file one frame at a time, so no frame is skipped and the slower, more accurate pose model can be used. This is the mode to use for numbers.

Timestamps are the file's own frame times where the browser reports them. The frame rate is measured from those times during a short play and can be corrected by hand. Stepping assumes a constant frame rate, so a variable-frame-rate recording may have frames repeated or missed. Slow-motion clips play back slower than real life: the slow-motion factor (for example 8 for 240 fps footage that plays at 30 fps) converts file time to real time, and velocities are wrong without it. Whether a file can be opened depends on the browser: H.264 MP4 and WebM are widely supported, and HEVC (.mov) clips from some phones are not.

Delayed view

The delayed view shows the picture, the overlays, the readouts and the live plot from a chosen number of seconds ago (5 to 60), so a person can perform a movement and then turn and watch it. The small picture in the corner is live. Everything shown comes from the same stored moment: the numbers were calculated when that frame was captured, with the same causal filter as the live estimates, and are replayed with the picture. The frame shown is the last one captured at or before the chosen moment, so the delay is accurate to about one frame interval.

Limits: the frames are held in the device's memory as compressed pictures, never uploaded. To keep memory use roughly level, longer delays store a smaller picture (width falls with the square root of the delay) and at most about 30 pictures a second, so at 60 frames per second the delayed picture and its skeleton update at half the camera's rate. The picture is therefore softer than the live one; measurements are unaffected, because they are made on the full-size live frame. Trials started while the delay is on record measurements from the live camera as usual, but no video and no key-moment screenshots, because the picture on screen is not the moment being measured. Changing the calibration or the angle convention restarts the delay rather than show stored numbers in the wrong units. The delay does not apply to video files.

Full screen view

Full screen shows the picture with, optionally, large measurement tiles and the live plot in a strip along the bottom. The numbers are the same live estimates as in the normal view (or the delayed or replayed ones, when those are on); nothing is calculated differently. Hiding a measurement in full screen removes its tile, its line on the plot and its angle drawing on the picture, but it is still measured and still recorded in a trial. Where the browser allows it, the screen is kept awake while full screen is on. On iPhone, which has no full screen mode for web pages, the view fills the browser window instead.

Planes of motion and axes of rotation

The three cardinal planes and the axis at right angles to each can be drawn on the person: sagittal plane and mediolateral axis, frontal plane and anteroposterior axis, transverse plane and longitudinal axis. Textbooks differ on axis names; the alternatives are listed beside the picture.

The direction the body faces comes from the pose model's estimated 3D landmarks. Depth is the model's least reliable output, so the heading is smoothed and is held exactly square-on when it is within about 10° of a front or side view. In side views, front and back are decided from the feet and face in the picture, not from depth. By default the long axis is the true vertical and only the heading follows the body; a second option tilts the planes with the trunk for lying or inverted positions. The planes are projected onto the picture without perspective. A small guide figure shows all three planes from a fixed angle, marks the plane the camera is looking at, and shows where the camera is relative to the body.

These are the textbook reference planes and axes described from the anatomical position. Real joint axes are oblique and move during motion.

Line of gravity, velocity vectors and joint moments

  • Line of gravity and base of support. A vertical line from the estimated centre of mass, and the span of the feet that are on the ground. Only the direction the camera can see is shown, and "vertical" assumes a level camera. A line of gravity outside the base means the posture cannot be held still; moving bodies are routinely in that state.
  • Velocity vectors. Each arrow shows where the point would be 0.15 s later at its present velocity. Dashed arrows are the tangential velocity v = ωr of the far end of a segment relative to its near end. These are live, causally filtered estimates.
  • Moment of gravity about a joint. The body beyond the joint is treated as one mass at its own centre of mass (segment masses and centres from de Leva, 1996). Moment = weight × horizontal distance from the joint. It is a static estimate: it ignores acceleration, so it is valid for held or slow postures only. For the hip, knee and ankle it assumes both legs share the load equally, and it needs a side view. The moment arm is measured in the picture plane.

Weight lifting mode

Finding the bar. The pose model does not recognise a barbell. The bar is located by following the weight plate, which appears as a circle in a side view. The app searches for circular edges (a gradient-voting Hough transform), then fits a circle to the rim to a fraction of a pixel. Only the outline is used, so a spinning plate does not disturb it. Because other round objects can be mistaken for a plate, the user confirms the plate with a tap. When the plate cannot be found in a frame, the bar is estimated from the wrists plus the last known offset, and that is marked on the picture and reported as a percentage of frames.

Calibration. A trial cannot start until the picture is calibrated and a load is entered. The plate diameter (450 mm for Olympic and bumper plates) is the preferred reference because the plate lies in the plane the bar moves in.

Calculations. Bar position is resampled, low-pass filtered without lag, and differentiated. With load mass m and g = 9.80665 m/s²:

  • Vertical force on the bar F = m (g + a).
  • Power delivered to the bar P = F v (plus the small horizontal term).
  • Work W = the sum of P over the lifting phase, which equals m g × rise plus the change in kinetic energy.
  • A lift runs from where the upward bar speed passes 3 % of its peak (at least 0.02 m/s) to where it falls below that again.
  • Mean propulsive velocity is the mean over the part of the lift with bar acceleration above -g (Sanchez-Medina et al., 2010).

What the numbers describe. These are values for the bar, not for the lifter's body. With body mass entered, "system" work and power use the combined centre of mass of lifter and bar from the segmental model, which is less certain.

How far to trust them. Range, time, mean velocity, work and mean power are robust. Peak acceleration, peak force and peak power come from differentiating twice and depend on frame rate and filter cutoff. At 30 frames per second fast lifts (cleans, snatches) are under-sampled and peaks are underestimated; the app warns when this applies. Every value carries the percentage error of the calibration.

Validation status. The calculations are checked against synthetic lifts with known answers, and the plate tracker against synthetic images and a synthetic barbell scene. The mode has not yet been compared with a linear position transducer or force plate on real lifts, and the tracker has not been tested on real gym footage.

What published comparisons report

Based on articles retrieved from PubMed, studies comparing this family of models with marker-based motion capture report results that depend strongly on the joint, the task and the camera view:

  • Hulleck et al. (2024) reported mean absolute errors of roughly 13° to 16° for the ankle angle during walking with BlazePose alone, improved by additional modelling. doi:10.1109/TNSRE.2024.3391908
  • Gao et al. (2025) found joint angle errors above 5° in children with cerebral palsy and judged the method suitable for screening rather than diagnosis. doi:10.1109/EMBC58623.2025.11254531
  • Hii et al. (2023) found good to excellent agreement for most temporal gait parameters. doi:10.3390/s23146489

These are examples, not a systematic review. The practical reading is that timing and large-range sagittal angles are the most trustworthy outputs, and small angles, distal joints and derivatives are the least.

Who the model works less well for

Pose models are trained on images of people and inherit the gaps in that data. Expect lower accuracy with loose or body-coloured clothing, long skirts or coats, unusual body positions, partial views, more than one person in frame, mobility aids and wheelchairs, prostheses and limb differences, and poor lighting or cluttered backgrounds. Low keypoint confidence is shown as grey dashed lines, and those values are excluded rather than guessed.
